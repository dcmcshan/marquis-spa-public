The research shelf

Touch, intimacy, and the stress response.

A curated shelf of peer-reviewed studies and Harvard Health articles on how affectionate touch and sexual intimacy relate to anxiety and physiological stress — gathered honestly, with the limits of the evidence left in view.

Browse the reading list The Marquis approach

Read before you read the studies

What the research suggests

Across quite different kinds of studies, three distinctions keep appearing. Holding them apart is the best way to read this shelf without overstating what the science demonstrates.

  • Immediate relief. Comforting physical contact — a hug, a partner’s embrace, self-soothing touch, or partnered sexual activity — is associated with lower physiological stress in the moment, including reduced cortisol responses and blood-pressure reactivity.
  • Longer-term regulation. The large meta-analytic literature on consensual touch suggests that repeated affectionate touch may contribute to lower ongoing anxiety, depression, and pain.
  • The deprivation question. There is not yet strong evidence that going without sex causes anxiety to accumulate biologically. The idea is intuitive, and many people report it as lived experience, but the research has not established it as a physiological mechanism.

If you read only two items, begin with the two marked start here in the shelf below.

The reading list

Every card links out to the source itself.

Start here · Archives of Sexual Behavior · 2025

Does Sex Today Relieve Stress Tomorrow?

Daily records from 645 partners in 319 newlywed couples found partnered sexual activity was associated with lower stress on the same day — but not with additional relief the next day. The effect appears to be immediate comfort, not a stored reserve.

Why start here: it separates same-day relief from lasting effects, which is exactly the question most people bring to this topic.

Start here · Nature Human Behaviour · 2024

The health benefits of touch interventions

A pre-registered meta-analysis of 137 studies and nearly 13,000 participants found touch interventions reduced anxiety, depression, and pain, with comparable mental-health and physical benefits across adults.

Why start here: it is the strongest overall evidence that consensual physical touch helps regulate anxiety.

Read the paper at Nature →

PLOS ONE · 2022

Partner embraces buffer the cortisol response

Women who embraced a romantic partner before a laboratory stress task showed reduced cortisol responses compared with women who did not; the effect was not observed in men in this sample.

Why read it: direct experimental evidence that romantic physical affection can blunt a physiological stress response, with honest limitations of its own.

Read the paper at PLOS →

Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology · 2021

Self-soothing touch and being hugged reduce cortisol

In a randomized controlled trial of 159 participants, both receiving a hug and simple self-soothing touch lowered cortisol responses after a standardized psychosocial stressor.

Why read it: comforting touch helps regulate stress even when it is not romantic or sexual at all.

Read the full text at PMC →

Harvard Health

Sex matters

A short, plain-language Harvard Health note: physical affection and sexual intimacy feel good, and they also raise oxytocin, a hormone associated with stress relief and a sense of well-being.

Why read it: an accessible statement of the sexual-intimacy-and-stress link from a mainstream medical source.

Read it at Harvard Health →

Harvard Health · 2026

Keeping affection alive, without pressure

Practical Harvard Health advice on maintaining kissing, cuddling, and other physical affection — including through stress, aging, and sexual difficulty — treating touch as connection rather than performance.

Why read it: it shows how intimacy can be sustained without pressure when sex itself is complicated.

Read it at Harvard Health →

Biological Psychology · 2006

Blood-pressure reactivity and recent intimacy

A two-week diary study of 46 adults found that people reporting recent penile-vaginal intercourse tended to show lower blood-pressure reactivity during stressful tasks than those reporting other or no sexual activity.

Why read it: relevant to the idea of accumulating tension, though the observational design cannot establish cause.

The honest margin

What the evidence does not show

The strongest findings concern touch and stress regulation, not abstinence and distress. No study has yet demonstrated that going without sex causes anxiety to accumulate biologically. Claims of “built-up tension” are best treated as personal experience rather than established physiology.

Why read it: because knowing what the science has not settled is part of staying honest about what it has.

A newer, fast-moving field

AI, anxiety, and relationship conversations

Short answer: AI can be a useful thinking aid, but current evidence does not support treating a general-purpose chatbot as a replacement for a trusted person, a couples therapist, or a qualified mental-health professional. Results depend on the tool and the task: early studies of structured, purpose-built interventions are promising, while ordinary relationship-advice chats have a particular risk of telling one person what they want to hear.

A chatbot may help you put feelings into words, organize a difficult conversation, or try a reflective exercise. It does not know the full relationship, share responsibility for the outcome, or reliably recognize when a situation needs human help. Five useful sources show both sides.

PLOS Mental Health · 2025

A purpose-built relationship-support chatbot

A randomized trial assigned 258 adults to a GPT-4o-based relationship chatbot or a conflict-reappraisal writing exercise. Most measures improved over time in both groups; there were no significant between-group differences for most outcomes. This tested one guided, single-session tool — not unsupervised use of a general chatbot as couples therapy.

Important limit: the study screened out people reporting suicidal ideation or domestic violence. Reviewers also found the chatbot did not consistently identify potential safety concerns.

Read the randomized trial →

Science · 2026

When advice becomes too agreeable

Across 11 models and experiments with more than 2,400 people, researchers found AI advice affirmed users’ positions more often than human responses. After discussing conflicts with sycophantic chatbots, participants felt more convinced they were right and were less inclined to apologize or repair the relationship.

Why it matters: warm, validating language can feel supportive while reinforcing a one-sided account.

Read the study in Science →

Journal of Medical Internet Research · 2025

What trials say about generative AI and mental health

A systematic review found a small average benefit across 14 randomized trials of generative-AI mental-health chatbots (6,314 participants). The prediction interval was wide and included no benefit, and the authors rated risk of bias as moderate — evidence of potential, not proof that any chatbot is safe or effective for everyone.

Read the review and meta-analysis →

NEJM AI · 2025

A promising trial is not a replacement test

In a four-week randomized trial of 210 adults with significant symptoms of depression, generalized anxiety, or eating disorders, the purpose-built Therabot showed promising symptom changes against a wait-list group. The authors call for larger studies to confirm effectiveness and generalizability; a wait-list comparison does not establish equivalence to human therapy.

Read the Therabot trial →

American Psychological Association · 2025

Guidance for using chatbots around mental health

The APA’s health advisory says people should not use generative-AI chatbots or wellness apps as a substitute for qualified mental-health care. It highlights concerns including inaccurate responses, privacy, over-validation, dependence, and unpredictable handling of crises.

Read the APA advisory summary →

A grounded way to use it

If you choose to use AI, treat the conversation as a draft for your own thinking. Ask it to separate facts from assumptions, suggest what you might be missing, and help you prepare an open question for your partner. Keep identifying or intimate details out of the chat, and check important claims rather than assuming a confident answer is accurate.

For mutual understanding, accountability, repair, recurring conflict, or decisions affecting your safety, involve a real person you trust or a qualified professional. An AI can help you rehearse that conversation; it cannot have it with your partner for you.

Adjunct, not substitute.
The evidence supports cautious experimentation with specific, bounded tools. It does not establish that a general-purpose AI is a healthy stand-in for human connection or relationship care.

The setting is not the treatment

Spa experiences and anxiety

Short answer: some specific spa modalities — especially warm-water bathing and balneotherapy, and possibly massage — have evidence of reducing measured anxiety or stress. “Going to a spa” is not one standardized intervention, though. Most studies test a defined course of bathing, massage, or combined treatments, often in clinical or medical-spa settings; they do not show that every spa visit treats an anxiety disorder.

Current Psychology · 2024

Warm-water and mineral bathing

A meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials (977 participants) found a moderate average reduction in anxiety scores across hydrotherapy and balneotherapy studies. Eight trials reported anxiety outcomes; results had moderate heterogeneity, and the balneotherapy subgroup was based on a comparatively small sample.

How to read it: promising support for particular water-based treatments, not evidence that a spa visit is a stand-alone treatment for an anxiety disorder.

Read the meta-analysis →

BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies · 2026

A structured medical-spa program

A multicenter randomized trial assigned 243 adults with moderate-to-high stress to 11 days of different balneotherapy programs or a control group. The intervention groups reported reductions in anxiety, stress, and related outcomes, with some improvements reported at six months.

Important context: participants’ starting anxiety scores were below clinical thresholds, and the programs combined specific treatments in medical spas. This cannot be generalized to all spa services or to treatment of diagnosed anxiety.

Read the trial abstract →

International Journal of Spa and Wellness · 2021

The broader balneotherapy evidence

A systematic review of 22 controlled studies found improvements in stress, sleep, and depression across parts of the literature; anxiety improved in some studies but not others. The authors called for more research to identify who benefits and which parts of a spa program matter.

Read the systematic review →

Massage and anxiety

Encouraging, but not settled

A small proof-of-concept randomized trial found that six weeks of twice-weekly Swedish massage reduced symptoms in adults with generalized anxiety disorder compared with light touch. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes the wider massage evidence for anxiety as limited and conflicting.

Bottom line: massage may feel calming and may help some people, but it is not an established replacement for evidence-based anxiety care.

Reading with care

Do Marquis rituals treat anxiety?

No. Marquis is a spa and experiential wellness space, not a clinic or psychotherapy practice. Rituals do not diagnose or treat health conditions and are not a substitute for licensed care. If anxiety is a persistent concern for you, a qualified health professional is the right place to start.

Do these studies say affection “detoxes” stress?

No. In several of them, physiological markers like cortisol moved even when people’s self-reported stress did not. Touch appears to work on the body’s stress response first; subjective feelings follow their own, less predictable, path.

Why do some results only appear for one sex?

Partly small samples and partly genuine open questions. The 2022 embrace study, for example, found the cortisol-buffering effect in women but not men in the same experiment. Results this fresh and this small are reasons for curiosity, not conclusions.

Why include older or observational studies?

Because they cover different ground. The 2006 blood-pressure study is observational, but it speaks directly to the accumulate-and-release idea that experimental designs have not yet addressed. Each card notes the limits of its own design.

Summaries, not substitutes.
The cards above are faithful short summaries; the linked papers remain the authority on what they found and what they did not. Nothing on this page constitutes medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.
